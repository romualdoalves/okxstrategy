# Abstract

Retail and semi-professional algorithmic trading platforms routinely deploy large catalogues of rule-based strategies whose published performance comes from backtests that are rarely audited end to end. We audit all 57 strategies of one such catalogue (trend-following, mean-reversion, price-action, scalping, regime and alternative-data families) on three spot pairs of a major exchange (BTC-USDT, ETH-USDT, SOL-USDT), using up to 8 years of daily and 1.9 to 4.6 years of intraday candles. The protocol combines a strict, cost-aware simulator (0.10% fee per side, 0.05% slippage per execution, closed candles only, no multi-timeframe look-ahead), a bootstrap lower bound on the profit factor, anchored walk-forward optimisation with a plateau test, and a source-level audit of each strategy.

Four results stand out. First, no intraday strategy survives: across 75 strategy-asset pairs on 1H, 15m and 5m data none has a bootstrap 5th-percentile profit factor above 1, and for pairs trading at least 500 times a year the mean net trade return is -0.34% of the stake against a modelled round-trip cost of 0.30%, i.e. a gross expectancy statistically indistinguishable from zero. Second, walk-forward parameter optimisation improves out-of-sample profit factor in only 12 of 24 strategy-asset cells and lowers aggregate out-of-sample profit by 3% relative to default parameters. Third, 4 of the 57 strategies (7%) could never open a position because of implementation defects, and 9 could not be tested at all (six depend on live-only data, three on timeframes with no usable history). Fourth, a discrepancy between the simulator and the live execution layer, a percentage cap on the initial stop that the simulator did not apply, lowered the median profit factor of five daily strategies from 2.30 to 0.94 and raised the number of trades of one strategy from 26 to 127; aligning the two restored the original ranking. Only three of the 57 strategies (all daily) are retained as candidates, and a family-wise calculation shows that even these are compatible with chance. We argue that parity between the simulated and executed trade lifecycle, and the explicit labelling of untestable strategies, deserve the same attention as out-of-sample design.

# 1. Introduction

The empirical literature on technical trading rules has long been dominated by a single concern: whether apparent profitability survives data snooping (Lo and MacKinlay, 1990; Sullivan, Timmermann and White, 1999; White, 2000; Hansen, 2005). More recent work shows that the problem is aggravated, not solved, by modern backtesting tools: the number of configurations a researcher can try is effectively unbounded, so the best in-sample result is a biased statistic (Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado and Zhu, 2014, 2017; Harvey, Liu and Zhu, 2016; Harvey, 2017). Cryptocurrency markets sharpen the question. They trade around the clock, are fragmented, and have attracted a large population of retail participants who use strategy marketplaces and bot platforms, yet the evidence on rule-based profitability is mixed and often ignores realistic costs (Corbet, Eraslan, Lucey and Sensoy, 2019; Gerritsen, Bouri, Ramezanifar and Roubaud, 2020; Hudson and Urquhart, 2021).

Most of this literature studies a small set of canonical rules, selected ex ante from textbooks. Practitioners face a different problem: an existing catalogue of dozens of strategies, written over time by different authors (or generated by tooling), each with free parameters, several documented only by prose, and all wired to an execution layer that adds its own risk management. The relevant question is not "do technical rules work?" but "which of the strategies we already run deserves capital, and is the evidence we have about it about the thing that actually executes?".

This paper reports a complete audit of such a catalogue and makes four contributions.

1. **A reproducible audit protocol** that combines a strict simulator, bootstrap lower bounds, anchored walk-forward optimisation with a plateau test, and a mandatory source-level review, and that assigns each strategy one of four verdicts (candidate, watch, retired, insufficient data), including an explicit verdict for strategies that cannot be tested.
2. **Evidence on the economics of frequency**: with realistic spot costs, the strategy families in the catalogue that trade on 5-minute to 1-hour bars have no measurable gross edge, and the relation between trading frequency and profit factor is negative and significant.
3. **Evidence on the limits of optimisation**: walk-forward selection does not beat default parameters on average, with a plateau test that rejects roughly half of the selected optima.
4. **A quantified case of simulator-execution divergence.** A single risk rule applied by the execution layer but not by the simulator moved the profit factor of every daily candidate by more than any parameter search in the study. We show how to detect and remove such divergence by placing the rule in a single shared module.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section 2 positions the study in the literature, Section 3 describes data and the trading environment, Section 4 the methodology, Section 5 the results, Section 6 discusses implications and limitations, and Section 7 concludes.

# 2. Related Literature

**Technical rules and data snooping.** Brock, Lakonishok and LeBaron (1992) documented predictive power of simple moving-average and breakout rules on the Dow Jones index; subsequent studies, summarised by Park and Irwin (2007), found that profitability declined after the 1990s and was fragile to costs and to data snooping. Sullivan, Timmermann and White (1999) and White (2000) provided bootstrap-based reality checks over the universe of rules actually considered, and Hansen (2005) improved their power. We follow the spirit of these tests, which account for the size of the search, but we apply them in a weaker form (Section 4.5) because the number of ex ante specifications is only partly observable in a legacy catalogue.

**Backtest overfitting.** Bailey et al. (2014) show that the minimum backtest length needed to avoid selecting spurious strategies grows rapidly with the number of trials, and Bailey and López de Prado (2014) deflate the Sharpe ratio for selection bias and non-normality. Bailey et al. (2017) define the probability of backtest overfitting through combinatorially symmetric cross-validation. Harvey and Liu (2015) and Harvey et al. (2016) argue for higher significance hurdles in light of multiple testing. Pardo (2008) and López de Prado (2018) describe walk-forward and purged cross-validation designs. Our contribution is not a new statistic but an applied audit in which each of these ideas is operationalised under an inspectable pipeline, and in which the *implementation* layer is examined with the same scepticism as the statistics.

**Cryptocurrency trading rules.** Corbet et al. (2019) find support for moving-average rules on high-frequency Bitcoin data, Gerritsen et al. (2020) find that a trading-range breakout rule beats buy-and-hold on a Sharpe basis in trending markets, and Hudson and Urquhart (2021), testing about 15,000 rules, report profitability that for Bitcoin disappears out of sample while persisting for other coins. Liu and Tsyvinski (2021) document momentum-related return predictors in the cross-section of coins. These studies typically evaluate rules in a frictionless or low-cost setting and treat the signal as the strategy; ours includes a full trade lifecycle (entry, stop, take-profit, trailing exit) and costs.

**Implementation risk.** The finance literature rarely treats software defects as a source of error in backtests, although practitioners know they are common. We document defects as data (Section 5.4) and show the effect of a simulator-execution mismatch (Section 5.5).

# 3. Data and Trading Environment

**Market and instruments.** All data are spot candles (open, high, low, close, volume) from a single exchange, for BTC-USDT, ETH-USDT and SOL-USDT. The exchange is a top-tier centralised venue with 0.10% taker fees at the standard tier, which we use for both sides of every trade.

**Coverage.** The candle history was assembled from the exchange's public API and an idempotent backfill process. Coverage by timeframe at the date of analysis was:

| Timeframe | BTC, ETH | SOL | Bars per series |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1D | 7.9 years (2018 onward) | 5.8 years (2021 onward) | about 2,900 |
| 4H | 3.6 years | 3.6 years | about 8,000 |
| 1H | 4.55 years (from April 2022) | 4.55 years | 40,000 |
| 15m | 4.3 years (from July 2022) | 4.3 years | 150,000 |
| 5m | 1.9 years (from December 2024) | 1.9 years | 200,000 |
| 1m | about 0.3 years | about 0.3 years | about 178,000 |
| 2m | none | none | none |

Strategies designed for 1-minute and 2-minute bars therefore cannot be tested (Section 5.1). The daily window contains exactly one pronounced bear market (2022: BTC -65%, ETH -68%, SOL -94%); the 2018 bear market falls inside the indicator warm-up period of the simulator. Survivorship is present by construction: the three assets are those that remain highly liquid today. Buy-and-hold returns over the daily samples were large (BTC +1,468%, ETH +1,405%, SOL +2,993% over the respective windows), so any strategy with market exposure below 100% is at a mechanical disadvantage in raw return and must be judged on risk-adjusted terms.

**Strategy catalogue.** The catalogue contains 57 strategies registered as source files, organised in families: trend-following (TF, 18), mean-reversion (MR, 8), price-action (PA, 16), scalping and order-flow (SC, 5), regime and graph models (RG, 5), information-flow models using on-chain, DEX or options data (IF, 3), a network-graph strategy (NW, 1) and one test strategy (T000). Each strategy exposes parameters with defaults, bounds and a recommended timeframe, and returns a signal (BUY, SELL, HOLD) together with metadata for stop-loss, first take-profit (TP1) and trailing distance. All strategies were written or generated before this study; their code was not changed during the study except where a defect was found and corrected, and every such change is reported.

# 4. Methodology

## 4.1 Simulator

The simulator is deliberately conservative and long-only, because the target market is spot: a SELL signal is interpreted as an exit of an open long position and is ignored when flat (it never opens a short). Key features:

- **Information set.** Only closed candles are used. At bar *t* the strategy sees a rolling window of the last 500 candles ending at *t*. Extra timeframes requested by a strategy (for example weekly candles for a daily strategy) are cut so that a higher-timeframe candle is visible only after it has closed, which removes the common look-ahead of using the still-forming weekly bar. A legacy mode reproduces the biased behaviour for before-and-after comparison.
- **Execution.** Entries occur at the close of the signal bar with adverse slippage of 0.05%; exits at stop, TP1-armed trailing, or signal close carry 0.05% adverse slippage. Fees are 0.10% of notional on each side. The round-trip cost is therefore 0.30% of notional.
- **Intrabar order of events.** When a stop and a favourable move could both occur within a bar, the stop is assumed to be hit first; trailing stops are updated with the bar's high before the low is tested. Both choices are pessimistic.
- **Position sizing.** A fixed stake of USD 100 per trade, not compounded, so that results are additive and comparable across assets.
- **Risk lifecycle.** The initial stop, TP1 and trailing distance come from the strategy's metadata, after two normalisations that mirror the live execution layer: a floor on stop distance of 1.5 ATR (with the target rescaled to preserve the strategy's reward-to-risk ratio) and a cap on stop distance (Section 5.5). TP1 arms a trailing stop of the strategy-defined distance.

## 4.2 Performance metrics

For each strategy-asset-timeframe series we report the number of trades and trades per year, win rate, net profit and maximum drawdown in USD, market exposure, the profit factor (PF = gross profit / gross loss, capped at 10 to prevent loss-free samples from dominating), and the buy-and-hold return and drawdown of the asset. Sampling uncertainty is addressed by a bootstrap (Efron, 1979): trade-level profits are resampled with replacement 2,000 times and we record the 5th percentile of the PF (*PF p5*), the median, and the probability that PF exceeds 1. The PF p5 is the primary statistic because it penalises small samples, which are the norm for daily strategies (1 to 35 trades per year). The bootstrap treats trades as exchangeable, which ignores serial dependence; we return to this in Section 6.2.

## 4.3 Baseline screening and out-of-sample split

Every strategy is first run once on every available asset at its recommended timeframe with default parameters ("baseline"). Trades are split by entry time: the last 30% of the evaluated bars form the out-of-sample (OOS) segment; an OOS result is considered conclusive only with at least 5 trades. Because default parameters are not tuned in this step, partitioning a single pass is equivalent to evaluating the two segments separately.

## 4.4 Anchored walk-forward optimisation

For strategies with a baseline that is not clearly negative, parameters are searched with anchored walk-forward analysis. The sample (after a 100-bar warm-up) is divided into four equal segments; for fold *k* = 1, 2, 3 the training set is segments 1 to *k* and the test set is segment *k* + 1. Search spaces are small grids defined per strategy (typically 3 to 5 values for 4 to 5 parameters, 54 to 80 combinations after random sampling with a fixed seed when the grid exceeds 120). Within each training window the combination with the highest bootstrap PF p5 is selected, subject to at least 8 training trades. The OOS performance of the selected combinations, concatenated across folds, is compared to the default parameters on the same test windows. A final candidate is chosen on the full sample, and a plateau test checks whether its neighbours (one parameter moved to the adjacent grid value) have PF above 1 and at least 70% of the candidate's PF; the optimum is considered stable if at least 60% of neighbours pass. The number of trials is reported in every case.

## 4.5 Verdict rules and multiple testing

Each strategy receives one of four verdicts:

- **Candidate:** positive OOS and baseline PF above 1 in at least two of three assets, with no asset showing material contradiction, and a parameter set that either is the default or converges across assets.
- **Watch:** promising but inconclusive (too few trades, mixed assets, or dependence on a single asset).
- **Retired:** losing at baseline on all assets (PF p5 well below 1 and low probability of PF > 1), or defective.
- **Insufficient data:** the strategy cannot be tested with the available history (live-only data or unavailable timeframe). No performance claim is made.

Verdicts were assigned by a human reviewer applying these written criteria; they are not the outcome of a formal hypothesis test. For the family-wise question, we use a back-of-the-envelope calculation: treating PF p5 > 1 as a one-sided 5% test of the null PF ≤ 1, the expected number of false positives among *m* independent strategy-asset pairs is 0.05*m*, to be compared with the observed count (Section 5.6). We did not apply a formal family-wise or false-discovery-rate correction (White, 2000; Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995), because the dependence between strategies is unknown and the bootstrap p5 is only an approximate test statistic; the calculation is meant as a plausibility check, not as an inference.

## 4.6 Source-level audit

Every strategy file was read and, where a result looked anomalous (no trades, extreme PF, results contradicting the strategy description), a diagnostic run was used to isolate the cause. Defects found and corrected are reported with their effect (Section 5.4). Strategies whose logic depends on data the simulator cannot reproduce (graphs built live, option-market snapshots, on-chain events, DEX quotes, wallet balances) are labelled "insufficient data" instead of being run with the inputs missing.

## 4.7 Simulator-execution parity check

After the statistical audit, we compared the risk lifecycle of the live execution layer with that of the simulator line by line. Any rule present in only one of the two was enumerated, and its effect was measured by re-running the baseline with and without it (Section 5.5). Where a rule was retained in the execution layer, it was moved to a module imported by both, so that a single implementation exists.

# 5. Results

## 5.1 The catalogue and the verdict distribution

Of the 57 strategies, 3 were retained as candidates, 9 placed on watch, 36 retired and 9 labelled insufficient data. By family (candidate / watch / retired / insufficient): TF 2/7/9/0; MR 0/1/7/0; PA 0/1/13/2; SC 0/0/4/1; RG 1/0/2/2; IF 0/0/1/2; NW 0/0/0/1; T000 0/0/0/1. Of the nine insufficient-data strategies, six require inputs that exist only live (two use an asset graph, one an option-market snapshot, one wallet balances, one on-chain events, one DEX quotes), one (PA009) is designed for 2-minute bars that the history does not contain, and two (PA012, T000) are designed for 1-minute bars with only 0.3 years available.

## 5.2 Intraday strategies: costs dominate

Table 1 summarises the intraday baselines (strategies run on their recommended timeframe with default parameters; pairs with zero trades excluded).

| Timeframe | Strategies | Pairs | Median PF | Pairs with PF > 1 | Pairs with PF p5 > 1 | Median trades per year |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1H | 4 | 12 | 0.67 | 2 | 0 | 44 |
| 15m | 15 | 45 | 0.50 | 1 | 0 | 269 |
| 5m | 6 | 18 | 0.35 | 0 | 0 | 83 |

No intraday pair has a PF p5 above 1. Among the 63 pairs with at least 30 trades, the Spearman rank correlation between trades per year and PF is -0.37 (p = 0.003). The pairs are not independent (the same strategy on three assets, overlapping data), so we read the p-value as descriptive, but the sign and magnitude are consistent across timeframes.

The mechanism is visible in the cost arithmetic. For the 24 pairs that trade at least 500 times a year, the mean net trade return, measured as profit divided by trades over the USD 100 stake, is -0.338%. The modelled round-trip cost is 0.20% in fees plus 0.10% in slippage, or 0.30%. The implied mean *gross* trade return is therefore about -0.04%, which is indistinguishable from zero given the dispersion of single-trade returns on these timeframes. In other words, these strategies are, on average, fair coins that pay a toll of 0.30% per round trip. At 800 to 1,700 trades per year, the toll alone is USD 240 to 510 per year on a USD 100 stake.

Low-frequency intraday strategies (6 to 45 trades per year) do not rescue the family: their samples are too small for a confident verdict and their PFs are mostly below 1 (for example PA005 on 1H: PF 1.20, 1.27 and 0.27 on BTC, SOL and ETH, with p5 below 0.32; placed on watch).

## 5.4 Implementation defects and untestable strategies

Source-level audit uncovered defects with first-order effects:

- **MR007** (power-law valuation, BTC only) produced zero trades because of two independent defects: a time attribute that did not exist in the candle structure caused the sell branch to run permanently, and the calibration intercept used the floor of the power-law channel (-17.584) rather than its central value (-17.01), so price was never below the "fair" level. After correcting both (the second to the published central value, without fitting to data), it produced 17 trades in 8 years with PF 5.03, about 70% of profit from 2020, and an OOS segment of 4 trades with PF 0.51: placed on watch.
- **PA008** (opening-range breakout with retest) never trades: the "opening range" is the first candles of the rolling window rather than the exchange's session open, and the retest requires a close simultaneously above and below the same line.
- **PA016** (three-impulse reversal) only emits sell signals (short entries by design) and therefore never opens a long position in a spot-only simulator.
- **RG004** (Markov regime graph) never trades. Its fourth criterion requires a 55% transition probability and a reward-to-risk of 2 against the nearest resistance; in a diagnostic run over 600 windows of synthetic data the criterion was never satisfied (0 of 600), and it was never satisfied on 4.3 years of real data.

Therefore 4 of 57 strategies (7%) could not open a position, including one that looked like the most extreme winner (MR007) once the first defect was fixed. This matters for catalogues in which strategies are selected by backtest score: a strategy with no trades has no losses and can look "safe" in an aggregate view.

A separate cost was computational. The Markov strategy's state-labelling loop re-sliced the data at each step (73 ms per call, about three hours per 150,000-bar series). Vectorising it reduced the cost to 3 ms and a test that compares the new implementation with the original loop on five parameter sets (including zero-range candles and NaN ATR) confirmed identical state sequences, bar by bar. Early timing numbers that suggested other strategies were prohibitively slow (850 ms per call) were artefacts of first-call warm-up and are a reminder to benchmark after warm-up.

## 5.5 A simulator-execution mismatch and its effect

Comparing the live execution layer with the simulator revealed four differences in the trade lifecycle:

1. The live layer capped the initial stop distance at min(4 ATR, 5% of price); the simulator did not cap it.
2. The live layer armed its trailing stop at +1% unrealised profit, whereas the simulator armed it when price reached the strategy's own TP1.
3. The live layer locked a breakeven stop at +0.4%, which does not exist in the simulator.
4. The live layer capped the trailing distance at 1.5% (decreasing with profit), whereas the simulator used the strategy's own trailing distance.

We aligned items 2 to 4 in the live layer to match the simulator, because the simulator's behaviour is the one the strategies were designed and tested under, and quantified item 1. The stop cap interacts with the strategy: TF008 and RG005 use a 3 ATR stop, which on daily BTC is wider than 5%. Table 3 reports the baseline PF and number of trades for the five daily strategies under three regimes: stop cap of min(4 ATR, 5%) (live rule at the time), min(4 ATR, 10%), and 4 ATR only.

| TF008 | ETH | 178 / 1.04 | 53 / 1.68 | 31 / 2.32 |
| TF008 | SOL | 194 / 1.13 | 77 / 1.63 | 21 / 4.36 |
| RG005 | BTC | 57 / 3.03 | 38 / 3.16 | 32 / 3.62 |
| RG005 | ETH | 83 / 0.64 | 37 / 2.53 | 28 / 4.17 |
| RG005 | SOL | 97 / 0.27 | 40 / 1.14 | 20 / 3.21 |
| TF009 | BTC / ETH / SOL | 31 / 0.88; 28 / 0.81; 21 / 0.88 | 31 / 1.29; 28 / 2.20; 21 / 0.80 | 31 / 1.27; 28 / 2.24; 21 / 2.09 |
| TF014 | BTC / ETH / SOL | 53 / 1.18; 53 / 1.96; 44 / 0.87 | 49 / 1.16; 48 / 1.56; 37 / 1.09 | 48 / 1.31; 46 / 1.60; 35 / 1.12 |
| TF017 | BTC / ETH / SOL | 28 / 1.20; 26 / 0.94; 19 / 0.07 | 28 / 2.61; 26 / 1.49; 19 / 1.87 | 28 / 2.54; 26 / 2.30; 19 / 1.42 |

Across the 15 pairs, the median PF is 0.94 under the 5% cap, 1.63 under the 10% cap and 2.30 with no percentage cap. The mechanism is a stop-out and re-entry loop: a cap tighter than the strategy's designed stop is hit by ordinary volatility, the entry signal is still true on the next bar, and the strategy re-enters, paying costs repeatedly (TF008 on BTC: 26 trades without the cap, 127 with it). Strategies that rarely re-enter (TF009) are affected less. This is a larger effect than any parameter search of Section 5.3, and it was invisible to every statistical safeguard because the simulator and the live layer disagreed about *what the strategy does*. We removed the 5% cap in both places, which means that stop distances up to 4 ATR (roughly 12% on daily BTC) are accepted, and placed the cap in a single module imported by both. A parameter in that module allows alternatives to be measured without code changes.

The corollary for statistical practice is that an out-of-sample design validates only the *pair* (strategy, simulator). If the simulator is not the executed system, the validation is about something else. At the time of writing we have not measured the effect of items 2 to 4 above in the simulator; we corrected them in the live layer instead, so the prior live performance (a small sample of Demo-account trades, 2 wins and 8 losses over a few days, with the stop cap in force) should not be read as evidence about the strategies.

## 5.6 Multiple testing

The audit evaluated 48 testable strategies on three assets, or 144 strategy-asset pairs. If PF p5 > 1 behaved as an exact one-sided 5% test and all nulls were true, we would expect about 7.2 positives. Among the 15 pairs of the five best daily strategies in the final regime, five have PF p5 above 1 (TF008 on BTC and SOL, RG005 on ETH, TF009 on ETH and TF017 on BTC), and no intraday pair clears the threshold. We did not retain a complete tabulation of the baselines of the other, retired, daily strategies in this regime, so the total count cannot be stated; they were retired because they lost at baseline, which makes it unlikely that they add many positives. The count we can document is therefore of the same order as the number expected under a global null. This calculation is crude (the bootstrap ignores serial dependence, strategies are correlated, and defaults for the candidate parameters were partly chosen on the same data), but it supports the cautious reading: the three candidates are hypotheses to be tested on forward data, not findings.

# 6. Discussion

## 6.1 Implications

**For practitioners.** Three practices follow directly. (i) Treat the catalogue as a population to be audited, not as a menu: 36 of 57 strategies lose money with realistic costs, and 4 do not trade at all. (ii) Before any parameter search, check for parity between the simulator and the executor: a single mismatched rule moved the median PF by a factor of 2.4 in our data. (iii) Report untestable strategies explicitly; an empty cell in a table of results otherwise reads as "not good" when it should read as "not known".

**For the cost-aware evaluation of technical rules.** Our intraday results illustrate the arithmetic of costs rather than a property of any rule: at 0.30% per round trip, a strategy needs a gross expectancy above that threshold per trade, which for 5-minute to 1-hour crypto rules appears to be well beyond what the families we tested deliver. This is consistent with studies reporting that profitability of rule-based trading in crypto weakens out of sample (Hudson and Urquhart, 2021) and with the general literature on declining technical-rule profitability (Park and Irwin, 2007). It does not exclude profitable strategies that use maker orders, lower fee tiers, or information beyond price.

**For optimisation.** Half of the walk-forward cells improved and the aggregate was slightly negative; the plateau test discarded roughly half of the selected optima. In this catalogue, default parameters are a strong baseline and optimisation is justified only where the optimum is convergent across assets and stable on a plateau.

## 6.2 Limitations

1. **Selection and data snooping.** We did not apply a formal family-wise or false-discovery-rate correction (Section 4.5). The count of positives is compatible with chance (Section 5.6), and the full-sample PFs of TF008 and RG005 benefit from parameter adoption on the same history.
2. **Bootstrap.** The bootstrap assumes exchangeable trades; stationary or block bootstraps (Politis and Romano, 1994) would be more appropriate where trades cluster in regimes, as they do here.
3. **Sample size and regime coverage.** Daily strategies have 1 to 35 trades per year, OOS segments of 2 to 10 trades for several cells, one bear market, and survivorship in the choice of three highly liquid assets (SOL covers only 5.8 years).
4. **Verdicts are human judgments** made under written but not pre-registered criteria. A blind re-assessment would be a useful robustness check.
5. **Fixed stake and non-compounded P&L.** We report USD results on a fixed stake and PF; we do not compute a time-series Sharpe ratio or compare equity curves with buy-and-hold on a volatility-matched basis.
6. **Parity is only partially verified.** We quantified one of four differences between simulator and executor; execution-layer effects that are not rules (latency, partial fills, order rejection) are not modelled, and a real order rejection due to a malformed size parameter was found and fixed during the study, confirming that such effects exist.
7. **Strategy fidelity.** Strategy source files were produced by an internal tool and several were produced by an internal strategy-generation tool and annotated as adaptations of published ideas; we audited their code against their own descriptions, not against the original authors' specifications, so a "retired" verdict applies to the implementation, not to the idea.
8. **Single venue and cost model.** Slippage is constant at 0.05%; at 5-minute frequency and on thinner books it is likely larger, which would only strengthen the intraday conclusion.

# 7. Conclusion

Auditing a complete catalogue of 57 rule-based strategies with a strict, cost-aware simulator, bootstrap lower bounds and walk-forward optimisation, we find that costs destroy the intraday families, that optimisation does not systematically help, that implementation defects are common, and that the simulator-executor mismatch can outweigh any statistical effect. Three daily strategies (TF008, RG005 and TF009) remain candidates, but their evidence is compatible with chance at the family level; their appropriate status is "hypotheses for forward testing". The audit protocol, the four-verdict taxonomy and the single-module treatment of shared risk rules are, we believe, transferable to other catalogues and venues. Future work should add a formal multiple-testing framework (reality-check or deflated-Sharpe style), a block bootstrap, volatility-matched benchmarking, maker-order execution models, and a forward test with pre-registered candidates.
